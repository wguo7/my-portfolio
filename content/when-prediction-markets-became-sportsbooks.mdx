## Summary

On January 23, 2025, Kalshi, the federally regulated exchange best known for markets on elections and economic data, officially launched sports betting. Within two months, sports made up more than half of everything traded on the platform.

This article asks a focused question: **did the arrival of sports bettors change how Kalshi's *non-sports* markets trade?** A user who signs up to bet on football can trade contracts on inflation or elections with the same account and the same balance, so habits formed in one product can carry into the other.

Using the platform's complete trade history, roughly 593 million trades from mid-2021 through August 2026, I find three things:

1. **Bets on unlikely outcomes surged.** Among contracts priced at 1 to 20 cents (outcomes the market considers less than 20 percent likely), the share of trades initiated to *buy* that unlikely outcome rose from 52.1 percent before the launch to 65.7 percent after. The change shows up in political markets, financial markets, and everything else.
2. **Those bets started losing much more.** In the cleanest comparison, markets that wrap up within 60 days, buyers of unlikely outcomes actually *made* 8.3 cents per dollar before the launch. Afterward they *lost* 21.6 cents per dollar.
3. **The total cost to those traders is real but hard to pin down precisely.** Compared with pre-launch loss rates, traders lost roughly $13 million more than expected after the launch. Because a few giant markets dominate the totals, that dollar figure carries wide uncertainty, and I present it as bookkeeping rather than as a statistical result.

I ran twelve headline tests and adjusted every result for the fact that twelve were run. Eight survive the adjustment. The ones that did not are reported anyway, because a result you can trust requires seeing what failed as well as what worked.

## 1. Why this question matters

The case for prediction markets is that their prices summarize what people collectively know. That works when the typical trader has a genuine opinion about the event. It works less well when the typical trader is buying the thrill of a long shot, the way lottery players do.

Betting research has a name for the lottery-player pattern: the **favorite-longshot bias**. Across racetracks and sportsbooks, people persistently pay too much for unlikely outcomes and too little for likely ones. How strongly a market shows this bias is a good indicator of who is trading in it.

That is what makes Kalshi's sports launch a natural experiment. Before 2025, the platform's users had chosen a forecasting site; they were, as this article shows, unusually careful about the prices of unlikely outcomes. The sports launch brought in a very different crowd, and nothing on the platform separates the two products. If sports-betting habits spilled over into the forecasting markets, three measurable things should change: which side of each trade people initiate, which prices they trade at, and how much money they subsequently make or lose. All three changed.

A note on vocabulary, kept deliberately minimal. On every trade, one side had an order sitting on the books and the other side came in and took it. I call the person who came in and took it the **initiator**. Initiators skew toward ordinary retail traders; the resting side skews toward professionals. When I say **unlikely outcomes** or **long shots**, I mean contracts priced at 1 to 20 cents, and "buying" one means initiating the trade that buys it.

## 2. The data

This analysis is built on what I believe is the largest public dataset of prediction-market activity, collected with an [open-source framework](https://github.com/Jon-Becker/prediction-market-analysis). After the refresh performed for this article, it contains about **593 million Kalshi trades and 30 million market records**, from June 2021 through August 4, 2026. Every trade shows who initiated it, at what price, and in what size; every market record shows how and when the market resolved.

Collecting the data surfaced three facts that shaped the study, and one of them should interest anyone who works with Kalshi data:

**Kalshi deletes its own history.** The platform's public data feed only returns trades from roughly the last 72 days; anything older is gone. I verified this directly: a market with millions of recorded trades returns data for May 26, 2026 and nothing for May 5, 2026. My dataset is complete through May 1, 2026, has a 23-day hole that can never be filled (the platform purged it before it could be collected), and is complete again from May 25 onward. If you want Kalshi's history, you have to record it continuously, as this project has since 2021.

**2026 Kalshi is a different platform.** By this summer, Kalshi listed tens of millions of tiny, minutes-long markets on cryptocurrency prices and was clearing about eight million trades per day, roughly one hundred times its 2024 pace. Data from this new era is not comparable to the older sample, both because the platform changed and because the collection method did. So **no statistical comparison in this article crosses May 2026**. All tests use the consistently collected sample ending May 1, 2026, updated with market outcomes through August (that update matters: thousands of markets that were still open in the spring have since settled, which sharpens the returns results). The new era gets its own section at the end, kept separate.

**Consistency rules.** All results use markets with at least 100 contracts of lifetime trading, the same rule used throughout the historical collection, so the set of markets being measured stays comparable over time. Deciding which markets count as "sports" uses a hand-curated list of ticker prefixes rather than keyword search; keyword search was tested and produced errors like classifying UKRAINE markets under weather because the name contains "RAIN".

## 3. How the comparison is set up

**The dividing line.** The before/after line is January 23, 2025, the official sports launch. A handful of sports markets technically existed from October 2024, but their volume was negligible. After the official launch, growth was explosive: sports passed 25 percent of all platform volume in mid-March 2025, passed 50 percent a week later, and peaked at 92 percent of weekly volume in late 2025.

![Kalshi sports weekly volume, cumulative share of post-launch sports volume, and sports share of total platform volume, 2024 to 2026](/img/blog/kalshi/sports-ramp.png)

**The election problem, addressed head-on.** The "before" period includes the 2024 U.S. election, the busiest and most retail-heavy stretch in the platform's pre-sports history. For the profit-and-loss results this stacks the deck *against* my findings: election-season losses make the "before" period look worse, which shrinks the measured deterioration afterward. As a check, I re-ran everything using only the twelve months before the launch as the "before" period. The shift toward unlikely outcomes gets *bigger* (+16.8 points instead of +13.6), and the returns deterioration remains large (−28.8 points, p = 0.003). Both versions are in the published results.

**How significance is assessed.** Weekly data of this kind has momentum: this week resembles last week, which can fool naive statistics. Every test here uses methods that account for that, and the dollar-loss figures are evaluated at the level of whole markets, because all the money in one market wins or loses together. Since I ran twelve headline tests, every reported significance level is also adjusted for having run twelve. Readers who want the exact procedures will find them in the methods note at the end; the full output, sixty tests including the failures, is in the public repository.

## 4. What changed

### 4.1 Money poured into unlikely outcomes

Take every non-sports trade where the "yes" side cost between 1 and 20 cents, and ask: did the trade's initiator buy the unlikely "yes", or bet against it?

Before the sports launch, initiators bought the unlikely side **52.1 percent** of the time, close to a coin flip. After the launch: **65.7 percent**. Adjusted for multiple testing, the probability of seeing a shift this large by chance is about one in a million.

![Share of initiators buying unlikely outcomes around the sports launch, and returns by price range before versus after](/img/blog/kalshi/composition-and-flb.png)

Three details establish that this is a platform-wide change in behavior rather than a quirk:

- **It appears everywhere.** Political markets shifted +8.2 points, financial and economic markets +9.9, and everything else +5.9, each strongly significant on its own.
- **It is not only the cheapest contracts.** Across the whole 1-to-99-cent price range, initiator buying tilted toward cheaper contracts after the launch, and that tilt is itself highly significant.
- **It is driven by new markets, not corrupted old ones.** Comparing each market only with itself (looking at markets that traded both before and after the launch, and accounting for each market's age), there is no detectable change: the estimate is −1.2 points, statistically indistinguishable from zero. The platform-wide shift comes from newly created markets and the flow arriving in them. That is exactly the fingerprint you would expect if the cause is new *people* and new *market types*, rather than existing markets going bad.

One more version of the result matters for later: restricting to markets that resolve within 60 days of creation, on both sides of the launch, the shift is +8.0 points and remains highly significant.

### 4.2 The losses on those bets roughly tripled

Here is the most surprising fact in the data, and it concerns the period *before* the launch. In fast-resolving markets (done within 60 days), people who bought unlikely outcomes on old Kalshi *made money*: +8.3 cents per dollar staked. The famous bias toward overpaying for long shots, found in nearly every betting market ever studied, essentially did not exist on the platform. Kalshi's early users were that price-conscious.

After the launch, the picture inverts:

| Comparison | Before | After | Read on significance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Week-by-week average return, buying at 1-20 cents | −2.5% | −42.2% | ~1 in 20,000 after adjustment |
| Return weighting every dollar equally | −37.8% | −47.6% | reported as description |
| Fast-resolving markets only (60 days) | +8.3% | −21.6% | ~1 in a million after adjustment |
| Counting only the period after Sept 2025 | | | significant (p = 0.003; conservative check p = 0.028) |

How to read this table. The first row is the formal statistical test, treating each week as one observation. The second row weights every dollar equally, which is the fairest measure of what traders actually experienced; it differs from the first because a few enormous election weeks dominate the dollar totals. The third row is the cleanest apples-to-apples comparison, and it shows the direction of the bias flipping from favorable to unfavorable. The fourth row shows the result still holds if the "after" period starts in September 2025, long after the election and at a point when sports made up about three quarters of platform volume.

Could the contracts themselves have gotten worse, rather than the buyers less careful? If unlikely outcomes had simply started happening less often, then whoever was on the *other* side of these same trades would show a mirror-image improvement for mechanical reasons. What actually happened is that the gap between the resting side's returns and the initiating side's returns widened sharply, which is the signature of a change in who is doing the buying, not a change in the events being bet on.

### 4.3 The bill, in dollars

Suppose post-launch traders had lost money at exactly the pre-launch rates, price bucket by price bucket. They would have lost about **$12.7 million** in non-sports markets after January 2025. They actually lost about **$25.7 million**. The difference, roughly **$13.0 million**, is the extra cost associated with the behavioral shift.

Where the excess comes from is itself telling. About $5.4 million sits in the 1-20 cent long-shot bucket. The single largest piece, $16.2 million, comes from the 61-80 cent range, where traders used to *make* money and now roughly break even: the old user base's edge on likely outcomes vanished along with its discipline on unlikely ones.

![Loss per dollar wagered by price range before versus after, and cumulative dollar losses on non-sports markets](/img/blog/kalshi/retail-losses.png)

I want to be precise about how much confidence these dollar figures deserve. An earlier draft attached extremely strong significance to them, using a calculation that quietly treated every dollar as its own independent data point. That was wrong: all the dollars riding on one market win or lose together, so the honest unit of evidence is the market, and there are far fewer markets than dollars. Evaluated that way, the $13 million excess could plausibly have arisen by chance (roughly a 1-in-4 probability), because a handful of very large markets dominate the totals. The behavioral results in 4.1 and 4.2 are the statistically solid findings. The dollar figure is the scale of loss they plausibly imply, and I label it bookkeeping, not proof.

## 5. Reasons to doubt, and how far they go

**The trend had already started.** Buying of unlikely outcomes was already drifting upward during 2024, before the launch line. Much of that drift rides the election cycle. Three checks limit how much of the result it can explain. In a quiet stretch of 2023, a fake "launch date" shows no effect at all (a placebo test, p = 0.66). Starting the "after" period in September 2025, far from the election, leaves the results intact. And dropping the largest markets entirely leaves the shift intact, so no single viral market is responsible. Someone who believes part of the change reflects election-era users lingering rather than sports bettors arriving has a defensible partial story; the checks above cap how much of the magnitude that story can claim.

**Why there is no comparison against Polymarket.** The natural design would compare Kalshi's markets with Polymarket's as a control group. I built that comparison and then discarded it, because the data itself rejected the premise: the two platforms were already on sharply different growth paths before the launch (a formal test rejects comparability decisively). Reporting a number from a comparison whose foundation fails would be worse than reporting none.

**Markets that haven't finished.** Profit calculations require knowing the outcome, and markets that hadn't resolved by the end of the sample drop out, which could distort things near the boundary. The 60-day-resolution comparison exists precisely to close this loophole, and the headline result survives it.

**Fees.** None of the return figures subtract Kalshi's trading fees. Including fees would make every number worse, so the reported deterioration is, if anything, understated.

## 6. The platform since May 2026, kept separate

The data after the 23-day gap describes yet another platform. Kalshi now clears about eight million trades per day. Competitive video gaming alone is about 23 percent of volume, and minutes-long cryptocurrency markets dominate the non-sports side. In this new era, initiators buy the unlikely side of long-shot markets **72.9 percent** of the time, higher than anything in the pre-gap sample, and those positions lose about 17 cents per dollar. Because the platform and the collection method both changed at the boundary, I present these as observations, not tests. Nothing in them suggests the patterns above are reversing.

## 7. What to take away

For its first four years, Kalshi's forecasting markets were traded by people who priced unlikely events carefully, so carefully that the most robust bias in betting research barely existed there. Within months of the sports launch, the flow of money into those same categories of markets looked like sportsbook flow: heavily tilted toward long shots, and paying heavily for it.

The change came through new markets and new participants rather than through decay of existing markets, which is consistent with the simplest explanation: the platform's new customers brought their habits with them. None of this necessarily damages prediction-market *prices*; professionals still keep quotes in line. But the experience of the ordinary trader worsened, measurably, at exactly the moment the industry found its mass-market product. Anyone weighing the social value of regulated event markets should hold both facts at once.

Everything here can be checked: the code, the data pipeline, and all sixty statistical results, including the ones that failed, are in the [public repository](https://github.com/Jon-Becker/prediction-market-analysis).

*Methods note, for technical readers. Before/after line: 2025-01-23. Weekly series tested with Newey-West (lag 4) regressions on a post indicator, with moving-block bootstrap p-values as a complement. Within-market comparison: market fixed effects, market-age control, standard errors clustered by market, absorbed effects counted in the degrees-of-freedom correction. Dollar aggregates: market-clustered bootstrap, 1,000 draws. Multiple testing: Holm-Bonferroni across the twelve-test headline family (eight survive). Returns follow Buergi, Deng and Whelan (2025): (payout − entry price) / entry price, dollar-weighted, initiator side taken from the aggressor flag on each trade. Sample: trades through 2026-05-01 under a stable 100-contract minimum-volume rule; resolutions as of 2026-08-04. Observational platform-level event study; causal readings are subject to Section 5.*
